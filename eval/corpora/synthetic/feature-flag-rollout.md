---
name: Progressive rollout procedure and the bucketing consistency requirement
description: The stage percentages and soak times for shipping behind a flag, why bucketing must be stable per subject, and the cleanup obligation that keeps flags from becoming permanent branches.
type: runbook
---
Anything that changes observable behaviour ships behind a flag and rolls out in stages. The ladder is one percent, five, twenty-five, fifty, then everything, with a soak at each stage. The soak is twenty-four hours for the first two stages and four hours thereafter, and the long early soak exists because the first stages are the only ones where a problem is small enough to be cheap and large enough to be visible. Going from one percent straight to fifty because the first stage looked fine is the most common way people get hurt here: at one percent many problems simply do not generate enough signal to distinguish from noise, so a clean first stage is weak evidence rather than strong.

Each stage has an explicit abort condition written down before the stage starts, and it must be a number on a graph rather than a judgement. Error rate for the affected endpoints, latency at the ninety-ninth percentile, and whatever business metric the change is supposed to move are the usual three. Writing the condition down in advance is what stops the rollout from being talked past a bad signal, which has happened and is a predictable consequence of the person watching the graph also being the person who wants the change to work.

Bucketing is by a stable subject identifier, hashed with the flag's own key, and both halves of that matter. Stable per subject means a given account is on the same side of the flag for every request, which is non-negotiable: a user flipping between old and new behaviour between page loads sees a broken product, and a cache keyed without regard to the flag will serve one variant's output to the other. Including the flag key in the hash means two independent flags at the same percentage do not select the same population, which they would if the hash were of the subject alone, and that correlation quietly destroys the independence any comparison between them assumes. Never bucket on a session or request identifier unless the change is genuinely invisible between requests.

A flag is a temporary branch and the cleanup is part of the work, not a follow-up. Every flag is registered with an owner and an expiry date at creation, the expiry defaults to ninety days, and an expired flag is reported weekly until it is removed. The reason for the enforcement is that a flag left in place is a permanent conditional that every future reader has to reason about and every future test has to cover twice, and the combinatorial cost of several of them is much larger than it looks. Removing a flag means deleting the losing branch and the flag definition, not setting the flag to a hundred percent and leaving the code.

Kill switches are the one legitimate exception and are marked as such at creation so the expiry report skips them. They are flags that exist to turn a subsystem off during an incident rather than to roll a change out, they default to on, and they are tested on a schedule because an untested kill switch is not a kill switch.
