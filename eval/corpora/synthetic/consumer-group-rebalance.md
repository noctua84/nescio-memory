---
name: Consumer group rebalance storm during the February backlog
description: Post-incident write-up of a rebalance loop triggered by slow message handling, why raising the poll interval was the wrong instinct, and the two settings that actually ended it.
type: incident
---
The symptom was that consumption throughput collapsed to near zero while every consumer appeared healthy. Nothing was crashing, no errors were being logged by the handlers, and the consumer processes were using plenty of processor time. The lag graph went vertical. What was actually happening was that the group was spending almost all of its time rebalancing: each consumer would be assigned partitions, begin processing, be evicted from the group before it finished, and the resulting membership change would trigger another rebalance that evicted somebody else.

The trigger was a change that made each message meaningfully slower to handle, because handling now involved an external call that had become slow under the same load. Each poll returned a batch of five hundred messages, and the consumer must return to the poll loop within the maximum poll interval or the coordinator considers it dead and reassigns its partitions. Five hundred messages at the new per-message cost exceeded that interval, so every consumer was evicted mid-batch, every eviction caused a rebalance, and every rebalance interrupted whatever progress the other consumers were making. It is a feedback loop, which is why it went from fine to total collapse with no middle ground.

The instinct in the room was to raise the maximum poll interval, and that instinct is wrong in a way worth understanding. It does stop the eviction, but it does so by making the group much slower to notice a consumer that has genuinely hung, which trades a visible problem for an invisible one. More importantly it does not address the cause: the batch is simply too large for the per-message cost, and if the per-message cost rises again the interval has to rise again.

What ended the incident was reducing the maximum records per poll so that a batch reliably completes inside the interval with room to spare. That is the setting that should be tuned, because it is the one that expresses the actual constraint, which is how much work a consumer takes on before checking back in. We set it low enough that a batch takes roughly a quarter of the interval at the worst per-message cost we had observed, which leaves headroom for the cost to degrade further without the loop restarting.

The second change was to separate the liveness signal from the processing loop, so that a consumer which is slow but working is distinguishable from one that has hung. With the heartbeat running independently, the session timeout can stay short and detect genuine failures quickly, while the poll interval governs only the much rarer case of a consumer stuck inside a batch. Finally, two operational notes: the rebalance was far more disruptive than it needed to be because the group was using the eager assignment strategy, which revokes every partition from every member on any change, and switching to a cooperative strategy means only the partitions that actually move are revoked. Offsets are committed after a batch, so every eviction also reprocessed the batch in flight, which is why the handlers must stay idempotent.
