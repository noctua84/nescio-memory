name: Object storage lifecycle rules and the incomplete upload leak
description: The tiering and expiry rules on the artefact bucket, plus the abandoned multipart upload problem that accounted for most of an unexplained storage bill.
type: runbook
---
The artefact bucket has three lifecycle rules and all three exist because of a specific bill. Objects move to infrequent access after thirty days, to archive storage after ninety, and expire entirely after four hundred days. The thirty day threshold is not arbitrary: the retrieval pattern we measured shows almost no reads after the fourth week, and the infrequent access tier is cheaper to store and more expensive to read, so the crossover sits around three weeks of no access. Thirty gives a margin.

The ninety day move to archive is the one that needs care, because archive retrieval is not merely more expensive, it is slow. A restore can take hours, and several teams have discovered this during an incident when they needed a build artefact from four months earlier and found they could not have it for the rest of the afternoon. If you depend on an object being available quickly beyond ninety days, the answer is to copy it somewhere else at creation time rather than to weaken the rule, because the rule is paying for itself many times over.

The expiry at four hundred days is set by the retention obligation plus a month of slack, and it should not be shortened without checking that obligation. It is also worth knowing that expiry applies to the current version only unless noncurrent version expiry is configured separately, which is the trap discussed below.

The genuinely surprising cost was none of these. An audit of an unexplained storage charge found that a large majority of the bytes being billed belonged to no visible object at all. They were parts of multipart uploads that had been started and never completed or aborted. A failed upload leaves its uploaded parts in place indefinitely, those parts are billed at full storage rates, and they do not appear in any object listing, so no amount of staring at the bucket contents will find them. Our uploader was retrying failed uploads by starting a fresh multipart upload rather than resuming or aborting the old one, which meant every transient network failure during a large upload leaked the parts uploaded so far.

The fix has two halves and you need both. The uploader now aborts explicitly on failure, which handles the common case. A lifecycle rule also aborts incomplete multipart uploads after seven days, which handles the case where the uploader dies before it can abort anything, and that case is exactly the one that occurs during the kind of incident where lots of uploads fail at once. Versioning interacts with all of this: with versioning enabled, deleting an object writes a delete marker and keeps the old version, which is billed. If a bucket has versioning on, it needs a noncurrent version expiry rule as well, or the expiry rules above only appear to be working.
