---
name: Invoice rounding and where tax is applied in the line item calculation
description: The decision to round per line rather than per invoice, how that interacts with percentage discounts, and the reconciliation difference that forced the rule to be written down.
type: decision
---
Rounding happens once per line item, to the currency's minor unit, after the discount is applied and before tax is calculated. Tax is then computed on the rounded discounted line amount, rounded itself, and the invoice total is the sum of already-rounded values. No rounding happens at the invoice level at all, which means the total is exactly the sum of what is printed and a customer adding up the lines by hand will always arrive at the printed total.

That last property is the whole reason for the rule and it is worth more than mathematical elegance. The alternative, carrying full precision through the calculation and rounding only the final total, produces a marginally more accurate total and an invoice whose lines do not add up to it. We ran that way for a year and the cost was a steady trickle of support contacts from customers who had added up the lines, plus a reconciliation process that had to explain a one or two unit difference on a meaningful fraction of invoices. The accuracy gained was worth far less than the trust lost.

Percentage discounts are where this gets genuinely tricky and where the two most recent bugs came from. A twenty percent discount on a line of three units at a price ending in an odd minor unit does not divide cleanly, and the question is whether to round the discount or the discounted amount. We round the discounted line amount, not the discount, because the discount is a derived quantity that nobody is entitled to see to the minor unit while the line amount is printed and must be exact. The consequence to be aware of is that the sum of the discounts across lines may not equal the discount percentage applied to the subtotal, and a reconciliation that checks that identity will report a difference that is not an error.

Rounding mode is half away from zero, which is what a person doing it by hand does and what every finance stakeholder expects. It is explicitly not banker's rounding, which rounds half to the nearest even value and is statistically better over many values but produces results that look wrong to a human checking one of them. This was argued and settled: the audience for an invoice is a person, and matching their expectation beats minimising aggregate drift.

Two implementation notes that have each caused an incident. Monetary values are stored and computed as integer minor units or as a fixed-point decimal, never as a binary floating point number, because a floating point representation of a currency amount accumulates error that eventually shows up as a total being one unit out with no traceable cause. And the tax rate applied to a line is the rate in effect on the invoice date rather than the current rate, which matters whenever a historical invoice is regenerated: regenerating last year's invoice with this year's rate produces a document that disagrees with what was actually charged.
